75 Smart challenge for doomscrolling

The TikTok challenge promises sharper focus and more mindful content consumption. After being shamed for her scroll-dominated screentime, one writer tries on a life more analogue.

'Mum, you're always on your phone.' These words from my seven-year-old daughter landed a gut-punch of pure, undiluted guilt. As beauty editor of Women's Health, I need to be extremely online. But somewhere between watching dermatologists dissect influencer nonsense and posting stories about pieces I've published, I fall into a scroll vortexgetting completely absorbed in scrolling, losing all track of time, one I can feel corroding my fruit fly-adjacent attention spanthe length of time you can concentrate on one thing.

So, when (ironic, I know) TikTok's algorithm began pushing its latest self-improvementactivities aimed at making yourself better or more successful obsession, the 75 Smart challenge, I felt both defensive and seen. Created by Sarah Lynn after she felt her brain turning to 'absolute mush', the 75-day challenge promises sharper cognitionthe ability to think clearly and understand things quickly and more mindfulpaying deliberate attention to the present moment; intentional content consumption. It's essentially the intellectual equivalent of the 75 Hard fitness challenge.

"The brain doesn't repair itself in noise — it repairs itself in quiet."

It's all peak analoguenot digital; using physical objects like paper instead of screens: audio-free morning walks, no social media between 9.30pm and 10am, reading a paper book daily, writing a morning page, no multitaskingdoing more than one thing at the same time media, seven hours' sleep, playing an instrument and learning a language thrice weekly. I discard language or instrument learning (I have two young kids) but replace with creative writing — and adhere to the rest.

The audio-free mornings were the easiest. I ditched podcasts and resisted firing off voice notes on dog walks. Initially, I felt bored, but plans began to form — and problems untangled.

'Constant scrolling, switching and notifications keep the nervous system in a low hum of alertness. But the brain doesn't repair itself in noise, it repairs itself in quiet. So, when you walk in silence, the brain's default mode network — the system linked to creativity, memory integration and meaning making — finally gets space to switch on.'

— Dr Jane Ollis, neuroscientist & founder of Sona

While stopping late-night scrolling matters and I did fall asleep more quickly, I didn't feel any brighter on waking. The no-social media before 10am rule was harder, as I often scroll on waking, so I deleted the app from my home screen and logged in only via my browser.

The beauty of the challenge is the way it encourages replacement of junk activities, not pure deprivationthe state of being without something you want or need, and I raced through novels. Handwriting my chaotic morning page felt slower, almost resistant at first. But, buoyed by Dr Velazquez who reminds me it activates language, emotional processing and motor pathways for the day ahead, I crack on.

Is this the silver bullet solution for digital dependence? No. Devices and the platforms on them are designed to keep users hookedaddicted; unable to stop using something, and the pushback shouldn't be down to individuals having to self-optimise our way out of mass manipulation. But having a set, long-term challenge — even if I'm yet to complete the full 75 days — has helped more than previous piecemealdone in small separate parts; incomplete or unsystematic efforts to chip away at my social media and phone use, and I do feel more in control of my attention.

'In the short term, you help cool and rebalance attentional networks that have been running too hot for too long. Long-term, you're creating the conditions for neuroplasticitythe brain's ability to change and form new connections through experience to reshape your brain.'

— Dr Velazquez, neuroscientist & scientific advisor, Mind Lab Pro
